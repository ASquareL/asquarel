DAY 6: DESIGN PRINCIPLES APPLIED

Study balance, contrast, emphasis, and hierarchy in real mobile design scenarios

Balance: Visual Weight Distribution

Symmetrical balance feels static. Asymmetrical balance feels dynamic. Both have applications. Understand the psychological difference and choose intentionally. A single large element on one side balances three small elements on the other.

On Mobile Screens

Mobile's narrow width favors asymmetrical balance. Use it strategically. Left-aligned text with right-aligned imagery creates movement. This movement guides eyes through your content naturally.

BALANCE PRINCIPLE: If your design feels lifeless, shift elements slightly off-center. Break symmetry. Create tension. Design is best when it has energy.

Contrast: The Attention Driver

High contrast commands attention. Low contrast creates subtlety. Color contrast, size contrast, weight contrast, style contrast—every design decision creates contrast or harmony with previous elements.

Hierarchy Through Contrast

Largest element attracts first. Next-largest second. This hierarchy guides viewers through your content in intended order. Without contrast, design becomes noise. With too much contrast, design becomes chaos.

Contrast Ratio Testing

Text needs 4.5:1 contrast ratio (WCAG AA). Large text needs 3:1. Measure contrast in design tools or use online calculators. Don't guess. This affects accessibility and legibility on mobile.

Emphasis: The Visual Focal Point

Every design needs one primary focal point. One thing viewers look at first. Size, color, position, isolation—use these to create emphasis. Secondary elements support. Tertiary elements complete the story.

Mobile-First Emphasis

On narrow screens, emphasis matters more. You can't hide important elements in peripheral space. Everything is either emphasized or ignored. Treat emphasis like real estate—it's precious.

Hierarchy: Information Architecture

Typography hierarchy, spatial hierarchy, color hierarchy—each creates meaning. Your call-to-action button needs more visual weight than disclaimer text. This isn't decoration—it's communication.

HIERARCHY RULE: Squint at your design. See four levels? Good. See eight levels? You've lost hierarchy. Viewers shouldn't work to understand what's important. Make it obvious.
